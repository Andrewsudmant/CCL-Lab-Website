# Site-level reader value

Gate 5F applies one McEnerney-informed rule across the site: public pages should create an argument for identifiable readers without displaying every editorial field as a separate labelled box.

The site must answer:

1. Which readers is the lab addressing?
2. What do those readers commonly assume about urban climate evidence and action?
3. Why does that understanding become unstable?
4. What consequence follows from treating the four questions separately?
5. What does the lab help readers understand differently?
6. Where are the limits of that improved understanding?
7. What should each reader do next on the website?

The principal reader communities are urban climate researchers; municipal, regional and national climate-policy practitioners; policy intermediaries, city networks and research organisations; and prospective students and research collaborators. The website does not write to an undifferentiated general public.

The principal claim is: **Urban climate evidence does not become useful merely because it exists.** Evidence becomes useful only when cities connect judgements about relevance across places, the value of producing new evidence, the institutions through which action is delivered and the consequences that follow for different people and places.

Treating those questions separately can make evidence appear more transferable, gaps more valuable, policies more implementable and benefits more evenly shared than they are. The lab therefore studies the questions as a cycle. This does not produce a universal transfer rule, ranking, recommendation engine or context-free best-practice list.

Structured fields remain in YAML for provenance, review and later correction. Public templates may combine or reorder them when doing so preserves meaning, boundaries and evidence status. Ongoing work uses prospective language; completed work uses source-backed language. Omit unsupported sections rather than filling them with generic prose.
