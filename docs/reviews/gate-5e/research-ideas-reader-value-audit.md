# Gate 5E research-ideas reader-value audit

Status: PASS

Twenty-four active records are present, six per theme. Every record below is owner-approved as a possible direction and retains the exact non-active/non-funded disclaimer; approval does not make it Work.

## Geographies of Climate Learning

### When similarity misleads (`g1-when-similarity-misleads`)

- **Problem of understanding:** Cities often select precedents through visible similarities such as population, climate or income, even though less visible institutional and infrastructural differences may alter the result.
- **Question:** Which contextual differences cause apparently comparable cities to draw different policy conclusions from the same evidence?
- **Consequence:** Analogue-based learning can create misplaced confidence when the characteristics used to define similarity are not the characteristics that govern the intervention or outcome.
- **Possible research design:** Matched-city comparisons could be combined with causal transportability analysis and sensitivity testing to identify which contextual variables alter the direction or magnitude of an inference.
- **Methods:** matched-city comparison, transportability analysis, causal generalisability, sensitivity analysis, hierarchical modelling.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

### Who becomes a reference city? (`g2-reference-city`)

- **Problem of understanding:** The most visible city cases may become influential because they are well documented or internationally connected rather than because their experience is broadly informative.
- **Question:** How do a small number of cities become influential reference cases, and how does their prominence shape what other cities regard as feasible?
- **Consequence:** Reliance on a small set of reference cities may narrow which policy pathways appear credible and reproduce the existing geography of urban climate knowledge.
- **Possible research design:** Citation-network analysis, city-network records and policy-document tracing could reconstruct how particular cases acquire authority and how their lessons are reproduced in guidance and municipal plans.
- **Methods:** citation-network analysis, policy-document tracing, network analysis, natural-language processing, comparative case analysis.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

### The missing geography of failure (`g3-missing-geography-failure`)

- **Problem of understanding:** Urban climate learning often relies on celebrated or completed cases, while abandoned and reversed interventions remain difficult to locate.
- **Question:** Are abandoned, reversed or unsuccessful urban climate interventions systematically absent from the evidence available to other cities?
- **Consequence:** A learning system built mainly from visible successes may overestimate feasibility and conceal the political or institutional conditions under which action fails.
- **Possible research design:** A registry of discontinued and unsuccessful initiatives, combined with archival research and comparative process tracing, could test whether documented success cases differ systematically from the wider population of interventions.
- **Methods:** archival research, case-registry development, comparative process tracing, selection-bias analysis, event-history analysis.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

### Evidence in translation (`g4-evidence-translation`)

- **Problem of understanding:** Cities rarely encounter research in its original scholarly form, yet little is known about how claims and limitations change as evidence is translated into guidance and plans.
- **Question:** How is urban climate evidence changed as it moves through city networks, consultancies, policy guidance and local decision processes?
- **Consequence:** Simplification or reframing may detach findings from the conditions under which they were produced and create stronger policy claims than the original evidence supports.
- **Possible research design:** Document-lineage analysis and text-similarity methods could trace changes between papers, guidance, consultancy reports and municipal plans, while interviews could explain why particular claims were retained or altered.
- **Methods:** document-lineage analysis, text similarity, natural-language processing, interviews, policy-document analysis.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

### Whose knowledge becomes admissible? (`g5-knowledge-admissibility`)

- **Problem of understanding:** Formal evidence hierarchies may exclude locally authoritative knowledge or incorporate it without preserving control over interpretation and use.
- **Question:** How do urban evidence systems recognise, subordinate or exclude Indigenous, community and practitioner knowledge?
- **Consequence:** Decisions may overlook place-specific conditions, while extractive forms of inclusion may reproduce inequities in who owns and authorises knowledge.
- **Possible research design:** Co-designed comparative research, undertaken only with appropriate partners and data-sovereignty arrangements, could examine how institutions define admissible evidence and how those rules affect decisions.
- **Methods:** co-designed research, institutional ethnography, comparative case study, governance analysis, knowledge-system mapping.
- **Required research-governance qualification:** Methods and research governance would need to be developed with appropriate Indigenous or community partners rather than specified unilaterally in advance.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

### AI-selected urban analogues (`g6-ai-urban-analogues`)

- **Problem of understanding:** AI-assisted discovery may increasingly shape which urban precedents researchers and practitioners encounter, but the geographical and contextual biases of those comparisons remain poorly understood.
- **Question:** When AI systems are asked which cities are comparable, which places do they recommend and which contextual differences do they ignore?
- **Consequence:** AI-generated analogues may reproduce existing evidence concentrations or recommend superficially similar cities while overlooking the conditions that determine relevance.
- **Possible research design:** Controlled prompt experiments, counterfactual city profiles and expert assessment could test the stability, geographical concentration and contextual adequacy of AI-generated city comparisons.
- **Methods:** controlled model experiments, counterfactual city profiles, audit studies, expert elicitation, geospatial analysis.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

## Where New Evidence Matters

### The next city worth studying (`e1-next-city`)

- **Problem of understanding:** Selecting an unstudied city because it fills a map gap may add little to collective understanding, while a strategically selected case could distinguish between competing mechanisms.
- **Question:** Which additional city case would most reduce decision-relevant uncertainty across a wider set of places?
- **Consequence:** Research resources could generate more cumulative knowledge if case selection considered how much a new case might change inference for other cities.
- **Possible research design:** Hierarchical models and value-of-information analysis, combined with active-learning approaches, could identify cases whose evidence would most change current estimates or classifications.
- **Methods:** hierarchical modelling, value-of-information analysis, active learning, optimal sample design, sensitivity analysis.
- **Required boundary:** This is an academic research question, not a proposal for a single public city-ranking or marginal-learning-value score.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

### When evaluation could change the decision (`e2-decision-changing-evaluation`)

- **Problem of understanding:** Evaluation resources are often directed toward visible programmes rather than decisions where uncertainty is actually pivotal.
- **Question:** Which urban climate policies sit close enough to an adoption, design or investment threshold that stronger causal evidence could change the decision?
- **Consequence:** A rigorous evaluation has greater practical value when its result could alter a live choice, rather than merely produce a better estimate after the decision is effectively settled.
- **Possible research design:** Decision-pathway mapping and threshold analysis could identify consequential uncertainties, followed by an assessment of where natural experiments, matched comparisons or synthetic controls are feasible.
- **Methods:** decision-pathway mapping, threshold analysis, natural experiments, difference-in-differences, synthetic controls.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

### Which disagreement deserves replication? (`e3-replication-disagreement`)

- **Problem of understanding:** Calls for replication often treat all findings similarly even though some disagreements concern inconsequential details and others destabilise widely used assumptions.
- **Question:** When urban climate studies reach conflicting conclusions, which disagreements reflect context, method or chance, and which are worth resolving?
- **Consequence:** Strategically targeted replication could clarify whether disagreement reflects genuine contextual heterogeneity or avoidable methodological differences.
- **Possible research design:** Structured replication portfolios, multiverse analysis and Bayesian model comparison could distinguish methodological disagreement from genuine contextual variation.
- **Methods:** coordinated replication, multiverse analysis, Bayesian model comparison, meta-analysis, research-design comparison.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

### When is there enough evidence? (`e4-enough-evidence`)

- **Problem of understanding:** Research fields may continue accumulating similar cases because data are accessible, even after the marginal contribution to understanding has declined.
- **Question:** At what point does another case study add little to understanding, and when should research effort shift to a different mechanism, population or place?
- **Consequence:** Without defensible stopping rules, research may reproduce well-covered questions while more consequential uncertainties remain unresolved.
- **Possible research design:** Sequential meta-analysis and explicit stopping rules could estimate diminishing informational returns, while sensitivity analysis could test whether apparent saturation hides neglected contexts.
- **Methods:** sequential meta-analysis, stopping-rule analysis, evidence-saturation analysis, sensitivity analysis, research-portfolio analysis.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

### Which exclusions change the answer? (`e5-consequential-exclusions`)

- **Problem of understanding:** Evidence maps can identify exclusions, but the number of missing places or populations does not show whether their inclusion would change the answer.
- **Question:** Which absent places or populations would materially alter an appraisal, causal estimate or claim about generalisability if they were represented?
- **Consequence:** Prioritising consequential exclusions could connect representational justice with the analytical effect of missing evidence.
- **Possible research design:** Evidence-gap mapping could be combined with population, vulnerability and policy data, followed by counterfactual reweighting and participatory priority setting.
- **Methods:** evidence-gap mapping, counterfactual reweighting, distributional analysis, participatory priority setting, scenario analysis.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

### A coordinated network of urban evaluations (`e6-coordinated-evaluations`)

- **Problem of understanding:** Individually useful studies may remain collectively incomparable because they measure different outcomes, use incompatible designs or repeat the same evidentiary limitations.
- **Question:** Could cities design complementary evaluations that answer a shared question rather than producing isolated case studies?
- **Consequence:** Coordinated evaluation could produce stronger cumulative evidence while retaining local adaptation and institutional relevance.
- **Possible research design:** An adaptive multi-city design could combine common outcome measures, staggered implementation, federated data and pre-agreed comparative hypotheses while allowing local variation.
- **Methods:** adaptive multi-site evaluation, federated analysis, staggered implementation, common outcome frameworks, comparative causal inference.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

## Modes of Climate Delivery

### Same policy, different delivery (`d1-same-policy-different-delivery`)

- **Problem of understanding:** Classifying climate action by intervention or policy instrument can conceal the mechanisms that make it operable in one city and ineffective in another.
- **Question:** Why do policies with the same formal label depend on different institutional configurations and follow different implementation trajectories?
- **Consequence:** Apparent policy similarity may mislead cities about which authority, finance, capabilities and relationships are required for implementation.
- **Possible research design:** Matched comparative cases and qualitative comparative analysis could identify recurring combinations of authority, finance, capability and participation, with process tracing used to test proposed mechanisms.
- **Methods:** matched case comparison, qualitative comparative analysis, process tracing, institutional mapping, configurational analysis.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

### Delivery across the policy lifecycle (`d2-delivery-lifecycle`)

- **Problem of understanding:** A configuration that secures initial adoption may not be capable of sustaining investment, maintaining infrastructure or adapting after political change.
- **Question:** How do delivery arrangements change between policy adoption, early implementation, expansion, maintenance and repair?
- **Consequence:** Treating delivery as a single implementation stage can hide why programmes weaken or require different institutional arrangements over time.
- **Possible research design:** Longitudinal process tracing and event-sequence analysis could reconstruct how responsibilities, resources and coalitions shift across a policy’s lifecycle.
- **Methods:** longitudinal process tracing, event-sequence analysis, policy-archive analysis, organisational interviews, implementation-history comparison.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

### Failure after adoption (`d3-failure-after-adoption`)

- **Problem of understanding:** Research frequently treats formal adoption as an outcome, leaving subsequent dilution, delay, suspension or abandonment poorly explained.
- **Question:** Why do technically credible and formally adopted urban climate policies stall before producing durable change?
- **Consequence:** Understanding post-adoption failure could reveal delivery conditions that are invisible when success is measured through policy commitment alone.
- **Possible research design:** Purposeful comparison of stalled and sustained programmes could combine bottleneck analysis, administrative records and interviews with actors positioned at key decision points.
- **Methods:** comparative failure analysis, bottleneck analysis, administrative-data analysis, process tracing, decision-point interviews.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

### Institutional substitution and its costs (`d4-institutional-substitution`)

- **Problem of understanding:** Non-municipal actors may enable action while changing accountability, public control, access or the distribution of risk.
- **Question:** When can utilities, businesses, community organisations or intermediaries compensate for limited municipal capacity, and what is lost when they do?
- **Consequence:** Institutional substitution should not be treated as an unqualified increase in capacity when it also redistributes authority and responsibility.
- **Possible research design:** Governance-network analysis and tracing of financial, legal and operational responsibilities could compare substituted functions across cities, complemented by analysis of legitimacy and distribution.
- **Methods:** governance-network analysis, responsibility mapping, comparative institutional analysis, financial-flow tracing, legitimacy analysis.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

### Multilevel climate delivery in Canada (`d5-canadian-multilevel-delivery`)

- **Problem of understanding:** Canadian municipalities often carry implementation responsibilities while depending on other governments for statutory authority, finance and programme continuity.
- **Question:** Under which federal–provincial–municipal arrangements do cities gain durable authority and resources for climate action, and when do they receive fragmented mandates instead?
- **Consequence:** Intergovernmental programmes may enable municipal delivery or transfer responsibility without the durable resources and powers required to act.
- **Possible research design:** Comparative federalism, intergovernmental fiscal analysis and paired municipal cases could distinguish enabling arrangements from fragmented mandates.
- **Methods:** comparative federalism, intergovernmental fiscal analysis, paired municipal cases, policy-network analysis, institutional analysis.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

### Participation that changes delivery (`d6-participation-changes-delivery`)

- **Problem of understanding:** Participation is frequently listed as part of delivery without establishing whether it changes the mechanism or distribution of decision-making.
- **Question:** When does public or community participation alter authority, resources or programme design, and when does it merely legitimate a predetermined decision?
- **Consequence:** Distinguishing consequential participation from procedural consultation could improve how delivery arrangements are compared and evaluated.
- **Possible research design:** Decision tracing across comparable programmes, combined with document analysis and interviews with participants and officials, could identify where participation changed choices and where it did not.
- **Methods:** decision tracing, comparative case study, document analysis, participant interviews, governance analysis.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

## Consequences for People and Places

### Distributional reversals over time (`c1-distributional-reversals`)

- **Problem of understanding:** Upfront subsidies, operating savings, property-price effects and tax or tariff changes may occur on different schedules and affect different populations.
- **Question:** Can a climate programme that appears progressive initially become regressive over time, or the reverse?
- **Consequence:** Static appraisal can misrepresent who ultimately gains or bears costs when the distribution of consequences changes across implementation stages.
- **Possible research design:** Longitudinal microsimulation and cohort analysis, supported by linked administrative or household data, could examine how incidence changes over time.
- **Methods:** longitudinal microsimulation, cohort analysis, linked administrative data, household-distribution analysis, scenario modelling.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

### The cumulative burden of policy packages (`c2-cumulative-policy-burden`)

- **Problem of understanding:** Policies are usually appraised individually even though households may experience overlapping transport costs, building requirements, energy-price changes and access benefits.
- **Question:** How do several climate policies interact within the same households and neighbourhoods?
- **Consequence:** A package that appears equitable policy by policy may produce concentrated cumulative burdens or benefits.
- **Possible research design:** Integrated household microsimulation and geospatial policy-package scenarios could estimate cumulative incidence and identify combinations that amplify or offset burdens.
- **Methods:** integrated microsimulation, geospatial incidence analysis, policy-package modelling, distributional appraisal, household scenario analysis.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

### Climate action and social cohesion (`c3-social-cohesion`)

- **Problem of understanding:** Social cohesion is often treated as a background condition for climate action rather than a consequence that policy itself can build or damage.
- **Question:** Under which conditions does climate action strengthen trust and collective capacity, and when does perceived unfairness erode them?
- **Consequence:** Policies that alter trust, reciprocity or collective capacity may affect both social outcomes and the durability of later climate action.
- **Possible research design:** Panel surveys, social-network measures and natural or quasi-experimental variation in programme exposure could be combined with comparative neighbourhood case studies.
- **Methods:** panel surveys, social-network analysis, natural experiments, neighbourhood comparison, mixed-method longitudinal research.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

### Where benefits stay and burdens move (`c4-benefits-stay-burdens-move`)

- **Problem of understanding:** A programme may appear locally beneficial while relying on distant labour, extractive supply chains or displaced environmental and social burdens.
- **Question:** When do urban climate investments retain employment, income, ownership and health benefits locally, and when do they shift costs or risks elsewhere?
- **Consequence:** Appraisal boundaries can make local gains visible while hiding consequences outside the city.
- **Possible research design:** Multi-regional input–output analysis, supply-chain tracing, ownership mapping and geospatial incidence analysis could connect local outcomes to consequences elsewhere.
- **Methods:** multi-regional input–output analysis, supply-chain tracing, ownership mapping, geospatial incidence analysis, territorial and consumption accounting.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

### Delivery-contingent co-benefits (`c5-delivery-contingent-co-benefits`)

- **Problem of understanding:** Two cities may adopt the same technology but produce different employment, health, affordability or cohesion outcomes because procurement, ownership, participation and financing differ.
- **Question:** Which wider benefits depend on how an intervention is delivered rather than on the intervention alone?
- **Consequence:** Treating co-benefits as properties of technologies can conceal the institutional mechanisms that create or distribute them.
- **Possible research design:** Matched intervention cases and mechanism-focused process evaluation could separate intervention effects from effects associated with delivery configuration.
- **Methods:** matched intervention comparison, mechanism-focused evaluation, mediation analysis, process tracing, mixed-method appraisal.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project

### Appraising what cannot be priced (`c6-unpriced-consequences`)

- **Problem of understanding:** Excluding difficult-to-price outcomes can make analysis appear more precise while systematically omitting effects that communities or decision-makers consider consequential.
- **Question:** How should urban climate appraisal represent adverse effects, deep uncertainty and outcomes that resist credible monetisation?
- **Consequence:** Appraisal methods shape which consequences enter a decision and which are rendered invisible.
- **Possible research design:** Probabilistic sensitivity analysis, robust decision-making, multi-criteria appraisal and deliberative valuation could be compared to determine what each reveals or conceals.
- **Methods:** probabilistic sensitivity analysis, robust decision-making, multi-criteria appraisal, deliberative valuation, threshold analysis.
- **Activity confirmation:** Research idea · not currently an active or funded project
