## Geographies of Climate Learning

### Final public copy

**Guiding question:** How does place shape which urban climate knowledge is produced, recognised and useful elsewhere?

Cities cannot investigate every climate question independently, so urban climate decisions necessarily draw on studies, models, cases and practical experience produced elsewhere. Yet research and policy often treat the existence of a precedent, or a visible resemblance between two cities, as evidence that its lessons will travel. That inference is uncertain. Institutional arrangements, infrastructure, climate, political authority, fiscal capacity, data systems and social conditions may alter what another city can plausibly learn.

Geographies of Climate Learning examines where urban climate knowledge is produced, which cities and populations are made visible, how particular places become influential reference cases, and which institutions recognise some forms of evidence as authoritative. It also follows evidence as it moves: how findings are interpreted, adapted, combined, contested or rejected in new settings. The aim is not to identify universally transferable lessons, but to understand what would have to be true for evidence produced in one place to become informative in another.

**Analytical boundary:** This theme concerns existing knowledge and the conditions under which it may be relevant elsewhere. It does not presume that proximity, similarity or the volume of available research establishes applicability.

**What this changes:** Cross-city learning is not simply a search for the most similar city. It is a judgement about which contextual differences matter for the decision at hand.

**Connection to the cycle:** Mapping the reach and limits of existing knowledge reveals which unresolved questions could be consequential enough to justify new research.

## Where New Evidence Matters

- **Stable understanding:** Important evidence is uneven and incomplete.
- **Instability:** An absence does not show that producing the missing evidence would be valuable.
- **Consequence:** Research resources may accumulate cases without changing consequential uncertainty.
- **Analytical intervention:** Connect evidence mapping to research design and decision analysis.
- **Changed understanding:** The size of an evidence gap and the value of filling it are different questions.
- **Boundary:** A data gap, new tool or under-studied location is not automatically a research priority. New evidence matters where its production could materially change understanding, appraisal or action.

### Final public copy

**Guiding question:** Which new evidence would most improve urban climate decisions?

Once the limits of existing evidence become visible, a further problem arises: deciding what evidence should be produced next. Urban climate research often treats an unstudied place, population or intervention as a research gap. Yet absence alone does not establish value. Some gaps may have little bearing on current decisions, while a strategically chosen case, causal evaluation, comparison or dataset may resolve uncertainty for many cities.

Where New Evidence Matters examines which additional knowledge could alter an appraisal, distinguish between competing explanations, clarify a mechanism, correct a consequential exclusion or expand the range of cities able to learn. It connects evidence mapping with research design and decision analysis, asking not simply where knowledge is missing, but where producing it could change what cities understand or do. The theme does not seek a universal ranking of research priorities; the value of new evidence depends on the decision, uncertainty and community it is intended to serve.

**Analytical boundary:** A data gap, new tool or under-studied location is not automatically a research priority. New evidence matters where its production could materially change understanding, appraisal or action.

**What this changes:** The size of an evidence gap and the value of filling it are different questions.

**Connection to the cycle:** New evidence matters most when it changes what decision-makers understand about how climate action can be delivered or what it may cause.

## Modes of Climate Delivery

- **Stable understanding:** Urban climate policy is often classified by targets, technologies or instruments.
- **Instability:** Evidence and ambition do not implement themselves, and similar policies can follow different trajectories.
- **Consequence:** Policy labels can conceal the authority, resources and capability required for durable action.
- **Analytical intervention:** Compare the institutional configurations through which change becomes operable.
- **Changed understanding:** The same intervention can represent a different form of climate action when authority, resources and responsibility are organised differently.
- **Boundary:** A delivery mode is a configuration through which change becomes operable and durable. It is not simply a technology, policy instrument, sector or list of participating actors.

### Final public copy

**Guiding question:** Through which institutional arrangements does urban climate action become possible and durable?

Urban climate policy is often described through its targets, technologies or formal instruments, as though implementation follows once an appropriate intervention has been selected. Yet evidence and ambition do not implement themselves. Climate action becomes operable through particular configurations of authority, finance, organisational capability, infrastructure provision, participation, coordination, sequencing and maintenance.

Modes of Climate Delivery compares how these configurations are assembled across city types, sectors and institutional settings. It asks why nominally similar policies follow different implementation trajectories, how responsibilities and resources shift over time, and which arrangements endure through political, fiscal or organisational change. The purpose is not to identify one preferred delivery model. It is to distinguish recurring mechanisms from context-dependent arrangements, so that apparent policy similarity does not conceal the institutions through which action actually occurs.

**Analytical boundary:** A delivery mode is a configuration through which change becomes operable and durable. It is not simply a technology, policy instrument, sector or list of participating actors.

**What this changes:** The same intervention can represent a different form of climate action when authority, resources and responsibility are organised differently.

**Connection to the cycle:** Delivery arrangements shape which consequences occur, who experiences them and whether climate action can be maintained over time.

## Consequences for People and Places

- **Stable understanding:** Climate action changes emissions and wider social, economic and health outcomes.
- **Instability:** Average benefits and total value can conceal different effects across people, places and periods.
- **Consequence:** Appraisal can hide burdens, harms, trade-offs and distributional change.
- **Analytical intervention:** Examine intended and unintended outcomes, their distribution and their dependence on delivery.
- **Changed understanding:** An intervention’s climate value cannot be separated from the distribution, timing and location of its wider consequences.
- **Boundary:** Consequences include intended and unintended effects, benefits and harms, and outcomes that cannot be reduced to a single monetary measure. The theme does not presume that climate action is automatically equitable or locally beneficial.

### Final public copy

**Guiding question:** What does climate action change, for whom, where and over what period?

Climate action changes more than greenhouse-gas emissions. It may alter health, affordability, mobility, employment, social cohesion, local economies, public services and exposure to risk. Yet appraisal often reports average benefits or total economic value, obscuring whether the same action produces different consequences across neighbourhoods, populations and periods.

Consequences for People and Places examines benefits, co-costs, burdens, adverse effects, trade-offs and distributional change. It asks who gains, who pays, where effects occur, how they develop over time and which consequences remain outside conventional appraisal. It also examines how delivery arrangements shape these outcomes. Consequences complete the learning cycle because they test prior assumptions, reveal unanticipated inequalities and change what other cities may plausibly infer from the intervention.

**Analytical boundary:** Consequences include intended and unintended effects, benefits and harms, and outcomes that cannot be reduced to a single monetary measure. The theme does not presume that climate action is automatically equitable or locally beneficial.

**What this changes:** An intervention’s climate value cannot be separated from the distribution, timing and location of its wider consequences.

**Connection to the cycle:** Observed consequences generate new knowledge, expose the limits of earlier assumptions and change what other cities may plausibly learn.
